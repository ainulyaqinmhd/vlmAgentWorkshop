# Gibson: Ecological Approach to Visual Perception

Workshop summary notes written for teaching. Not a substitute for the original book.

## Ambient Optic Array
The ambient optic array is the structured pattern of light that reaches an observer from every direction at a given point of observation. Gibson argued that this structure, not a flat retinal image, carries the information we use to perceive the environment. In a sketch, the optic array is suggested by how surfaces, edges and openings are arranged around the viewer's station point. A drawing that conveys the optic array well lets the viewer feel where they are standing and what surrounds them.

## Texture Gradients
A texture gradient is the gradual change in the density, size and spacing of surface texture with distance. Paving stones, windows or grass appear larger and more widely spaced near the observer and smaller and denser further away. Texture gradients specify the slant and distance of surfaces without needing explicit perspective lines. In sketching, drawing texture with decreasing size and increasing density toward the horizon is one of the strongest cues for depth along the ground plane.

## Optic Flow
Optic flow is the pattern of motion in the optic array produced when an observer moves. When moving forward, the scene appears to expand outward from a single point, the focus of expansion, which specifies the direction of travel. A static sketch can imply optic flow through converging lines of a street, a path leading into the scene, or elements arranged to radiate from a vanishing point. Strong implied optic flow invites the viewer to imagine moving through the space.

## Affordances
An affordance is what the environment offers an animal for action: a flat, rigid, knee-high surface affords sitting; a gap wider than the body affords passing through; stairs afford climbing. Affordances are relational, depending on both the environment and the observer's body and abilities. In an urban sketch, showing benches, doorways, steps and paths at believable human scale lets a viewer perceive what the place affords. Including human figures helps calibrate scale and makes affordances easier to read.

## Invariants
Invariants are properties of the optic array that remain constant while other properties change, for example while the observer moves or lighting shifts. The horizon ratio is a classic invariant: the horizon cuts all objects of the same height at the same proportion, regardless of distance. Invariants let observers perceive stable properties such as size, shape and layout. A sketch that keeps consistent eye level and proportions across the scene respects these invariants and feels spatially coherent.

## Occlusion and Layout
Occlusion happens when a nearer surface hides part of a farther one. The edge where one surface covers another, the occluding edge, specifies which surface is in front. Gibson emphasised that occlusion reveals the layout of surfaces and that moving the head reveals or hides surfaces progressively. In drawing, clear overlapping of buildings, trees and figures is a reliable way to build layered depth from foreground to background.

## Surfaces and the Ground Plane
For Gibson, perception is fundamentally about surfaces: their layout, texture, and how they meet. The ground plane is the most important surface because all other objects rest on it and are located relative to it. Objects are perceived as farther away when their base is higher in the visual field, closer to the horizon. A sketch that establishes a clear, continuous ground plane gives every other element a place to stand.
