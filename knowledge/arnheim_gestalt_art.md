# Arnheim and Gestalt: Composition in Art

Workshop summary notes written for teaching. Not a substitute for the original books.

## Visual Balance
Arnheim described balance as a state in which the perceptual forces in a composition compensate for one another, so the image appears stable and complete. Balance is not the same as symmetry; an asymmetric composition can balance a large light shape against a small dark one. An unbalanced composition looks accidental or unfinished, unless the imbalance is clearly intentional. When critiquing, ask whether any part of the picture seems to pull the whole image off centre.

## Visual Weight
Visual weight is how strongly an element attracts attention and influences balance. Weight increases with size, darkness or saturation, isolation, intrinsic interest such as faces, and distance from the centre. Elements higher in the frame and on the right side are often perceived as heavier. Artists adjust weight to direct the viewer's gaze and to balance the composition.

## Structural Skeleton
Arnheim proposed that every frame has a hidden structural skeleton: the centre, the vertical and horizontal axes, and the diagonals. Elements placed on these lines feel stable, while elements slightly off them create tension as if they want to move. The skeleton explains why a shape just off centre feels restless and one exactly centred feels calm. Composers use it deliberately to create either rest or movement.

## Directed Tension
Directed tension is the sense of movement or pull within a static image. Oblique lines, wedge shapes, gazes and gestures all create directional forces that the eye follows. Diagonals are more dynamic than horizontals and verticals. A composition with well-organised tension leads the viewer through the image along a deliberate path.

## Figure and Ground
Gestalt psychology describes how the visual field is organised into figure, the shape that stands out, and ground, the surrounding area. Smaller, enclosed, convex and higher-contrast regions tend to be seen as figure. Ambiguous figure-ground relationships can be used for effect, but unintentional ambiguity confuses the reading of an image. Clear value contrast between subject and background is the simplest way to establish figure.

## Proximity and Similarity
The Gestalt law of proximity says elements close together are perceived as a group. The law of similarity says elements that share colour, shape, size or texture are perceived as related. Artists use these laws to create clusters, rhythms and visual families across a composition. Breaking similarity with one different element creates a focal point.

## Closure and Good Continuation
Closure is the tendency to perceive incomplete shapes as whole, filling in gaps. Good continuation is the tendency to follow smooth lines and curves rather than abrupt changes in direction. Sketches rely heavily on both: a few suggestive lines can imply a complete form. Lost-and-found edges use closure to keep a drawing lively while staying readable.

## Pragnanz (Simplicity)
The law of Pragnanz states that perception tends toward the simplest, most regular organisation that the stimulus allows. Viewers prefer to see a configuration as a few simple shapes rather than many complex ones. Strong compositions often reduce to a small number of clear large shapes, a principle sometimes called the notan or thumbnail structure. When a composition feels busy, simplifying the big shapes usually helps more than adding detail.
